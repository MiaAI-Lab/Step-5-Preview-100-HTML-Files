Why the ecliptic

The Sun and the planets all travel within a few degrees of the same great circle. Map in coordinates that follow the Earth's tilt and their paths become straight lines, which is why the zodiac is a band and not a scatter.

ii

Parallax

Watch a nearby star for a year and it draws a tiny ellipse against the distant ones: the orbit of the Earth turned into a ruler. One parsec is the distance where that ellipse is one arcsecond across, and every distance here is parsecs.

iii

Precession

The Earth's axis turns once in about 26,000 years, so the equinox crawls westward through the zodiac by roughly 50 arcseconds a year. A star's ecliptic longitude grows, slowly, and this chart can show it.

iv

Bayer and Flamsteed

Johann Bayer lettered the bright stars of each constellation in brightness order, Alpha through Omega; John Flamsteed numbered them left to right in right ascension. Both are printed beside the stars they name.

v

Magnitude

Magnitude is logarithmic and backward: five steps is a factor of a hundred. The area of a star's disc on this chart falls off with magnitude, the way your eye does.

vi

Latitude

Pull the latitude to your own and the whole chart pivots: the ecliptic's tilt, the horizon, and which constellations ever clear it all follow. Drag to pan and scroll to zoom.
