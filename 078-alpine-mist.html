The valley fills
from the bottom

Fog in a mountain valley is not cloud coming down. It is cloud that never lifted, sitting in the cold air that drains off the slopes overnight — and the height it reaches is exactly the height where the air stops being cold enough to hold it.

i

The lapse rate

Temperature falls about 6.5 degrees for every kilometre you climb. That single number is why the valley can be in fog while the ridge above it is in sun, and why the snow line sits at a predictable height.

ii

The inversion

Fog means the coldest air is at the bottom, which is the opposite of the normal arrangement and the reason the whole thing inverts: warm air is sitting on top of cold air that has nowhere to go.

iii

The snow line

Snow survives above the height where the air stays at or below freezing. Drag the cloud base down and you will see it: the same peaks, the same snow, and the fog simply not reaching them.

iv

Clear ridges

The number in the corner is how many of the five ridges are visible above the fog, which is a real measure of a day on the hill and the first thing a forecast tells you.

v

Sun angle

Light from the left rakes across the ridges and leaves them in shadow on the right. Move the sun and the whole picture re-lights, including the fog, which brightens from the side the light is coming from.

vi

Wind

Wind tears the top off the fog and pushes it through the gaps, which is the one thing that reliably clears a valley: not warmth, but movement.
