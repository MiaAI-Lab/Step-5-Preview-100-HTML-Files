i

Nucleation

A bubble will not start in smooth liquid, because a surface takes energy to open. It starts on a scratch, a speck of cellulose or a fibre left by the cloth — which is why the chain always comes from the etched point.

ii

The wobble

Drag flattens the leading face, and past a certain size the whole thing starts to oscillate, shedding a wake. That wake is what leaves the little trail of tiny bubbles under every big one.

iii

Pressure

A bottle holds about six atmospheres, most of it carbon dioxide that only stays dissolved while there is nowhere for it to go. Open it and the pressure goes to one, and the gas starts leaving.

iv

The flute

A narrow glass keeps the bubbles in a column and gives the carbon dioxide nowhere to escape sideways, so the pour lasts longer than in a coupe. Shape is physics.

v

Temperature

Cold dissolves more and releases it more slowly, which is why a chilled flute fizzes steadily for minutes and a warm one is over in seconds.

vi

Flat

Let it go flat and the CO₂ leaves the solution entirely: the wake thins out, the chains stop and the glass is only wine. Nothing is wrong; the gas has simply left.
