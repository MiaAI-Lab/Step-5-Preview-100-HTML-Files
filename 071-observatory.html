i

The field

Everything in the eyepiece is a projection: right ascension across, declination up, with the whole sheet turning as the earth does. Slew and the field slides underneath the fixed reticle.

ii

Altitude

How high a target sits depends on where you are and what time it is. Cross the meridian and you get the best view you will get that night; lose it below the horizon and you wait for the next one.

iii

Field of view

A wide field finds things and shows context. A narrow one resolves them but makes you hunt. The frame drawn in the eyepiece is the real field at the magnification you chose.

iv

Magnitude

Each step is about two and a half times brighter, and the scale is backwards, so magnitude six is roughly a hundred times fainter than magnitude one. The stars in the field are drawn at their real relative brightness.

v

Tracking

Track the sky and the mount follows the field rate on its polar axis, which is why a long exposure is possible at all. Stop tracking and the sky goes streaky — here it goes streaky by blur.

vi

Red light

The whole page is dark because dark adaptation is real: it takes half an hour to get and one white light to lose, which is why observatories are lit in red.
