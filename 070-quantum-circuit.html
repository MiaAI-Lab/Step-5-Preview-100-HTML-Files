A qubit is a point
on a sphere

Not a bit with a question mark. It is a vector, and every gate is a rotation of that vector. Place gates on the wires, run the circuit, and watch where each one ends up pointing.

Amplitudes after the run

i

The sphere

The north pole is |0⟩, the south is |1⟩, and the equator is an equal superposition with a phase that depends on where round the middle you sit. A gate that leaves the axis alone is doing nothing.

ii

Rotations

X is a half turn about the horizontal axis through |0⟩ and |1⟩. H is a half turn about an axis in the middle of those, which is why it turns |0⟩ into something that is neither.

iii

Measurement

The vertical coordinate decides the odds: the probability of |1⟩ is how far down the vector points. Measure and the vector jumps to one of the poles and stays there, which is why you only get one shot.

iv

One qubit each

This bench draws a Bloch vector per wire and rotates it directly, which is exact for single-qubit work. The two-qubit control is a simplification: it reads the wire above and flips this one if that one is down.

v

Phases

Z flips the phase on |1⟩ and S turns it a quarter step. Neither is visible in the probabilities — only in where the vector sits around the equator, and therefore in what a following gate will do.

vi

Idempotence

Applying the same half turn twice brings you back. That is the whole reason the X X pair in step 1 and 5 cancels, and why real circuits are written to avoid it.
