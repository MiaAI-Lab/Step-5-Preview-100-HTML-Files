A cut is the only
way to make it shine

A rough stone is a lump of glass. What makes it a gem is the cut: pavilions angled to keep the light inside, bouncing it to the crown and back out at you, which is why the right facet angle is worth more than the stone itself.

i

Total internal reflection

Light leaving a dense medium past the critical angle cannot get out, so it turns back in. Diamond's is 24 degrees, ruby's is 33, and that single number is why a diamond's pavilion is cut where it is.

ii

Dispersion

The index is not one number, it is a curve. Red is bent least and violet most, so a cut that spreads the rays spreads the colour too — and fire is the spectrum coming out the top.

iii

Yield

The cut throws away most of the rough. A round brilliant keeps about a third of a stone's weight, which is why a fine gem is mostly the cutter's decision and only partly the mine's.

iv

The pavilion

Too shallow and the light goes straight out the bottom and the stone looks dead. Too steep and it leaks out the side and looks dark. The window is two degrees wide and the slider is in it.

v

The table

The flat top is where the light is supposed to leave. Widen it and more comes out at once and the stone looks bigger; narrow it and the pattern tightens and the fire concentrates.

vi

Fire

Show the fire and each ray is drawn in the colour it actually comes out at, split by the dispersion of this species. The same cut, the same light, a different spectrum.
