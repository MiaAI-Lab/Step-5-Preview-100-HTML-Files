# SIGHT REDUCTION — a line of position from two stars

## Short title
Star Navigation

## The exact prompt used to generate this HTML file

Create a completely self-contained single-file HTML page: a sextant sight reduced into a
circle of position on a plotting sheet, with the real corrections — dip, index error and
refraction — applied to the reading. No external dependencies whatsoever — no CDN, no web
fonts, no external images, no JS libraries. All CSS and JavaScript inline, works offline
by double-clicking, responsive, valid HTML5.

Artistic direction: a night deck. Ground #0a1016, hairlines rgba(230,238,246,.14), body
#e6eef6 with muted #93a4b4, brass #e8c46a as the accent and a cold cyan #7fd4ff for the
figures. Serif headings with italic accent words, uppercase .2em–.4em kickers, monospace
figures.

The deck is one canvas: a night sky, the observed star at its measured altitude above a
summed-sine sea, a sextant drawn at the pivot — an indexed arc with a degree scale, an
index arm carrying the mirror, a half-silvered horizon glass at the centre, and the index
error drawn as the dashed offset between the mirror's line and the star. Inset bottom-right
is a plotting sheet.

The plotting sheet is an offscreen canvas: a graticule of longitude and latitude lines, and
each sight drawn as a circle of position centred on that star's substellar point, with the
radius from the zenith distance. Two circles cross and the crossing is marked with a
reticle, and the readout says whether you have one circle or a crossing.

The corrections are the real ones. Dip is 1.76 times the square root of the height of eye
in metres, in minutes of arc, so four metres is about 3.6 minutes and it is always
subtracted. Index error is a fixed offset you measured at the dock, in minutes of arc.
Refraction uses Bennett's formula for the observed altitude. The corrected altitude, the
zenith distance, and the distance to the substellar point in nautical miles — one minute
of arc is one nautical mile — all fall out of that.

Controls: eight bodies to observe, a mounting row (steady, heaving, rolling), the star's
altitude, the swell, the index error, Take the sight and Clear the chart. Readout: measured
altitude, corrected altitude, observer height, dip, zenith distance, and the fix state.

Layout: nav, centred hero, the deck canvas with the reading overlay, a dock of two chip
rows, three sliders and two buttons, a readout row, then two three-cell note rows on dip,
index error, intercept, the instrument, swell and the chart, then a footer.

Interaction: choose a body, set its altitude, add an index error and watch the dashed line
move, and take the sight — the circle appears on the sheet. Take a second and a third and
the crossing appears; the readout changes from one circle to crossing. Set the mounting to
rolling and the frame rocks while the reading stays put, which is the point of the
instrument.

## One-paragraph description of the intended design
A sextant on a night deck, drawn as an indexed arc with a degree scale, an index arm, a
half-silvered horizon glass and a dashed index-error line over a summed-sine sea — with an
inset plotting sheet where each sight becomes a circle of position on a graticule centred
on the star's substellar point and two circles crossing into a marked fix — and where dip,
index error and Bennett refraction are the real corrections applied to the reading, and the
distance to the substellar point is the zenith distance in minutes of arc read as nautical
miles.

## Major visual techniques used
- Indexed arc drawn from the pivot with its own degree scale and ticks, so the
  instrument reads as a measuring device
- Index error as the dashed offset between the mirror's line and the star, so the
  correction is visible rather than asserted
- Dip as 1.76 times the square root of height of eye in metres, in minutes of arc
- Index error in minutes of arc, subtracted from the measured altitude
- Refraction by Bennett's formula on the observed altitude, in minutes of arc
- Distance to the substellar point as the zenith distance in minutes of arc, because one
  nautical mile is one minute of arc — the unit equivalence the whole method rests on
- Sea as three summed sines, with swell lines offset downward from the same function
- Plotting sheet as an offscreen canvas clipped into the corner of the main canvas
- Circles of position with radius from the zenith distance, each centred on its star's
  substellar point placed at a different azimuth
- Fix reticle drawn only when there are two or more circles
- Mounting row that rocks the frame around the pivot while the reading is unchanged,
  which is the whole reason a sextant beats a quadrant
- Eight bodies with their own glare colour, from the Sun to Acrux
- Eight-body palette as a single shade per body, so a change repaints the star

## Intended interaction model
A sight to reduce. Visitors pick a body, set its altitude, add the index error they would
have measured at the dock, and take the sight; a circle appears on the plotting sheet. Take
a second and the crossing appears and the readout changes. Rock the mounting to see that
the reading does not move, and clear the sheet for a new set.
