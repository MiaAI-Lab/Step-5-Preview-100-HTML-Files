The case for
doing less,
more slowly.

Every generation has believed itself the busiest that ever lived. This one may be right — and that is precisely why the argument for slowness has to be made again, and made differently.

Words by Ines Okonkwo · Photography by R. Halloran

There is a particular quality to the light in a workshop at four in the afternoon, when the work has been going badly all day and nobody has said so. The bench is warm. The tools have been picked up and put down enough times that their handles have taken the shape of one pair of hands, and the noise of the street has become a kind of weather rather than an event.

What is happening in the room looks, from the outside, like nothing. That is the difficulty and the whole argument at once. Slowness is invisible in the way that health is invisible: you notice it only when it has gone.

Against the tyranny of the visible

We have built an entire economy of signals — the reply, the reaction, the story — and every one of them rewards speed. Nothing in the system pays for the thing that only a long afternoon can produce, because the long afternoon produces exactly nothing that can be shown.

The economics

An hour of attention withdrawn from a screen is an hour of attention withdrawn from a market. This is why slowness is not merely unfashionable; it is structurally punished.

And yet the market has no answer to the question of who is willing to spend eleven days finishing a movement for a watch that eighty people will buy. The answer is: someone who has decided that the eleven days are the product.

What slowness is not

It is not leisure, which is its own kind of consumption. It is not nostalgia. It is certainly not the romantic poverty of people who have simply not been offered anything better.

Slowness is a technical decision about the order in which things are allowed to happen. It is the willingness to leave a sentence wrong for a week, to let a joint dry before it is cut, to hold a disagreement for a month instead of settling it badly in an evening.

Every craftsperson describes this the same way: the work tells you what it wants, but only if you are still in the room when it does. The room has to be slow enough that the telling is possible.

The fastest way to do something well is to be willing to do it badly for a long time first.
Marguerite Adeyemi, potter, in conversation
