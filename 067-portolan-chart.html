A chart drawn
the way they were

Not a map of anywhere. It is a map of how maps were made: coastlines inked from a log, a compass rose with sixteen wind names, rhumb lines radiating to the edge of the paper, and a route drawn as a dashed guess.

i

The rose

Sixteen points named for winds, not for numbers. North is at the top because the pole star was the only fixed thing available, and every other direction was a name before it was an angle.

ii

Rhumb lines

Straight lines from the rose to the edge of the paper, one for every point. They are what a navigator actually followed: a constant bearing, drawn as a ruler across the sea.

iii

The route

A dashed line between the places, crawling along itself. Portolan charts drew courses as dashes, and the length of the dash was a rough measure of a day's sail.

iv

Faded

The whole chart is one ink colour whose weight and transparency you set. Faded means the paper has aged where the sun hits it, which is the only difference between a new chart and an old one.

v

Mountains

Hachures, not contours — short strokes following the slope, so the height is read from the density of the marks rather than from a number.

vi

The blank

Where the chart stops is not the edge of the world, it is the edge of what anyone had sailed. Old charts left the rest blank and drew a monster in the margin.
