Bioluminescence is not light storage. A cell makes a luciferin, an enzyme oxidises it, and the reaction emits a photon — and in these dinoflagellates the trigger is mechanical, so a paddle, a wave or a hand will do.

i

The chemistry

A luciferin is oxidised by a luciferase in the presence of oxygen, and the spare energy comes out as a photon. The reaction is cold, efficient, and it needs no light to charge it — which is why nothing here has to be exposed first.

ii

The trigger

In these cells it is a shear force on the membrane, which opens an ion channel and drops the pH, which activates the luciferase. Mechanical, immediate, and gone in a tenth of a second.

iii

Blue

Almost everything in the sea glows blue, because blue is the wavelength that travels furthest through water and the one the eye of a shallow predator is tuned to. Deep red would be invisible within a few metres.

iv

The bloom

A bloom is a bloom: millions of cells per litre, visible from a boat as a dull red brown by day and a bright blue by night. Density here is the number of cells you can see.

v

Afterglow

The reaction is fast, so a cell's own emission is short. The afterglow you see is the wave passing through the rest of the bloom and setting off the cells it has not reached yet.

vi

Still water

Still water is dark water. Nothing flashes, nothing spreads, and the only thing to look at is the bloom itself — which is what the bay is like when nothing disturbs it.
