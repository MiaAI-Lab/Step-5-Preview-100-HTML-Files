The last twenty minutes
are worth the whole day

When the sun is a few degrees up, its light has a hundred times more air to climb through than at noon, and the air takes the blue out of it. What reaches the canyon wall is red, warm, and almost horizontal.

i

Air mass

One air mass is exactly the thickness of atmosphere the sun crosses at noon. At six degrees it is nearly ten, and every extra mass takes more blue out of the beam, so the light is redder the lower the sun sits.

ii

Colour temperature

Noonlight is around 5,500 kelvin. Sunset is around 2,000. The gap is not a filter, it is what the air did to the spectrum on the way through it.

iii

Shadows

A six-degree sun casts a shadow almost ten times the height of the thing making it. On a canyon wall that means the whole face goes dark except a few lit terraces, which is why the last light is the flattering one.

iv

Long shadows

Dust and haze lengthen the path and deepen the red, but they also cut the direct light, so the walls flatten. The best canyon sunsets are the ones after a rain, when there is colour without the haze.

v

Four rocks

Navajo sandstone is pale and goes rose. Redwall limestone is grey and warms to orange. Chinle shale is violet. Basalt stays dark all day and only glows at the very end.

vi

Three skies

Clear, high cloud and low cloud each change where the light comes from: clear is one point, high cloud spreads it across a bright band, low cloud makes a red underglow with no sun at all.
