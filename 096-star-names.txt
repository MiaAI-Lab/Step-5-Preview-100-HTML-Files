# STAR NAMES — a sky you can carry north

## Short title
Star Names

## The exact prompt used to generate this HTML file

Create a completely self-contained single-file HTML page: a real star chart of the
ecliptic in true coordinates, where the twelve months place the Sun in the zodiac, where
a star's parallax circle is drawn in the right size, and where the equinox drift is shown
as it really happens. No external dependencies whatsoever — no CDN, no web fonts, no
external images, no JS libraries. All CSS and JavaScript inline, works offline by
double-clicking, responsive, valid HTML5.

Artistic direction: a cold, deep night sky. Ground #070a12, hairlines rgba(230,236,247,.13),
body #e6ecf7 with muted #93a3bd, starlight blue #9fc4ff as the accent and sun yellow #ffd68c
for the Sun. Serif headings with italic accent words, uppercase .2em–.4em kickers,
monospace figures.

Fifty-six real stars across thirteen ecliptic and zodiac constellations, each with its
name, abbreviation, right ascension, declination, apparent magnitude and distance in
parsecs: Aldebaran, Pollux, Regulus, Spica, Antares, Rasalhague, Kaus Australis, Deneb
Algedi, Sadalsuud and the rest.

The chart is an orthographic projection of the celestial sphere centred on a chosen right
ascension and declination, so the horizon is the limb of the visible hemisphere and stars
below it are simply not drawn. Declination follows the latitude slider, which is why the
whole sky pivots. The ecliptic is a great circle computed from the obliquity of 23.439°,
and the zodiac band is ±8° of ecliptic latitude around it.

Ecliptic and equatorial coordinates are both computed exactly. Parallax is 1/d arcseconds,
so Alpha Centauri's 1.3 parsecs give 0.75″ and Antares's 169 parsecs give 0.006″. Precession
moves a star's ecliptic longitude by 50.29 arcseconds a year, so over a full cycle of 26,000
years the year counter runs the equinox right round the zodiac.

Controls: twelve months, five drawing toggles (figures, ecliptic, zodiac, parallax,
precession), latitude, sidereal time, faint limit, Tonight, Show precession. Drag to pan,
scroll to zoom. Readout: month, stars drawn, nearest star, second-nearest parallax,
constellations, and a caption line giving latitude, right ascension, declination and the
active exaggeration.

Layout: nav, centred hero, the sky canvas with its caption beside a control column, then
two three-cell note rows on the ecliptic, parallax, precession, Bayer and Flamsteed,
magnitude and latitude, then a footer.

Interaction: the chart opens on the evening sky for January. Visitors pull the latitude to
their own and watch the whole hemisphere pivot and the constellations below the horizon
disappear; step the months round and watch the Sun travel through the zodiac one sign at a
time; turn on parallax and see a ring round every star sized by 1/d; press Show precession
and watch the drag arrows grow as the year counter climbs toward 26,000; drag to pan the
sky and scroll to zoom in on a figure.

## One-paragraph description of the intended design
A real star chart of the ecliptic on canvas, drawn by orthographic projection of the
celestial sphere so the horizon is the limb of the visible hemisphere and declination
follows the latitude slider — where fifty-six named stars in thirteen constellations carry
their true right ascension, declination, magnitude and distance in parsecs, the ecliptic is
a great circle at the obliquity of 23.439°, the zodiac band is ±8° of ecliptic latitude,
parallax rings are 1/d arcseconds, and precession moves the stars 50.29 arcseconds of
longitude a year, with drag to pan, scroll to zoom, and a Tonight button that centres the
actual sky on the visitor's actual date.

## Major visual techniques used
- Orthographic projection of the celestial sphere centred on a chosen right ascension and
  declination, so the horizon is the limb and hidden stars are not drawn
- The ecliptic as a true great circle at the obliquity of 23.439°, and the zodiac band as
  ±8° of ecliptic latitude
- Exact transforms between equatorial and ecliptic coordinates
- Parallax as 1/d arcseconds, drawn as rings exaggerated ×10⁵ so they are visible
- Precession as 50.29 arcseconds of longitude a year, running a full 26,000-year cycle
- Star radius from magnitude, so five magnitudes is half the disc, the way the eye works
- Star colour banded by magnitude, warm for the brightest and cool for the faintest
- Constellation stick figures as ordered lists of star names, clipped at the horizon
- The Sun placed in its zodiac sign for the month, with a radial glow and a label
- A dashed equinox meridian and a dashed celestial equator
- A caption line in the corner giving the latitude, right ascension, declination and any
  active exaggeration

## Intended interaction model
A sky to carry north. Visitors set their own latitude and watch the hemisphere pivot and
the constellations south of the horizon vanish; step the months round and watch the Sun
walk through the zodiac a sign at a time; switch on parallax and read a ring's size as a
distance; press Show precession and watch the drag arrows lengthen as the year counter
climbs toward 26,000; drag to pan and scroll to zoom into a single figure; and press
Tonight to see the sky as it actually is tonight.
