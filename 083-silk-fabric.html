A weave is not a texture, it is two families of yarn crossing on a fixed rule. Change the rule and the same dyed yarn becomes poplin, becomes twill, becomes satin — and catches the light on a completely different axis.

i

Plain weave

One over, one under, both ways. The simplest interlacing there is, and the reason it is the strongest and the dullest: no yarn floats long enough to catch a highlight, so the surface stays matte.

ii

Twill

One over two, shifted one thread each row, gives a diagonal ridge. The floats lie in one direction and the light follows them, which is why denim fades along its own grain.

iii

Satin

Four or more over, one under, with the tie points scattered so nothing lines up. The long floats are what make the gloss, and they are also why it snags.

iv

The bolt

Thread count is how many threads fit in a square inch. Low is a cheesecloth and high is a handkerchief, and this bolt goes from 24 to 480.

v

Slub

Real yarn is not a tube. Random thick and thin sections along each thread are the irregularity that makes linen look like linen and cotton look like cotton.

vi

The fold

Fold the bolt and a cast shadow crosses the cloth; drape it and the whole sheet takes a slow wave. Both are the same picture with the geometry changed.
