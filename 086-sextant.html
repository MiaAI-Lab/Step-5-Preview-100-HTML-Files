Measuring the altitude of a star does not put you on a point. It puts you somewhere on a circle a thousand miles across, centred where that star is straight overhead. Two stars, two circles, and the crossing is where you are.

i

The dip

The horizon you see is below the true horizon, and by how much depends on your height of eye. Four metres above the water is about 3.6 minutes of arc, and every sextant observation starts by correcting for it.

ii

Index error

The instrument is never perfectly zeroed, and its error is a fixed offset that you measure at the dock and subtract from every sight. The slider is in minutes of arc, because that is the unit it is specified in.

iii

Intercept

Each circle drawn from a sight is a thousand miles across, so a second and third one do not meet in a dot — they cross in a small triangle, and that triangle is your fix, its size your uncertainty.

iv

The instrument

The sextant measures an angle between what the index mirror shows you and what the horizon glass shows you, so the reading is the star's height above the horizon — and the frame can move under you without changing it.

v

Swell

The horizon is not a line, it is a bank of water. On a swell you take the mean of the crests by eye, which is why a real observation is a set of readings you average, not one.

vi

The chart

Sights are drawn on a plotting sheet as circles with the substellar point at the centre. Two circles cross twice; you are at the one near your dead reckoning.
