# RIPPLE TANK — a wave equation you can disturb

## Short title
Wave Grid

## The exact prompt used to generate this HTML file

Create a completely self-contained single-file HTML page: a ripple tank — a height field
integrated on a grid, with reflecting edges, driven sources and a hand you can drag through
it. No external dependencies whatsoever — no CDN, no web fonts, no external images, no JS
libraries. All CSS and JavaScript inline, works offline by double-clicking, responsive,
valid HTML5.

Artistic direction: a lit tank in a dark room. Ground #070a12, canvas #04060c, hairlines
rgba(233,241,255,.14), body #e9f1ff with muted #93a4bd, cyan #5fd0e8 for the accent and
pink #ff8fd0 for the figures. Serif headings with italic accent words, uppercase .2em–.4em
kickers, monospace figures.

The tank is two Float32Arrays of 200 × 112 and one update: each cell becomes the mean of
its four neighbours minus its own previous value, times a damping factor. Two arrays, one
swap, no trigonometry — and the result refracts, reflects and interferes, because that is
what the equation means. The edges are handled by copying the row or column just inside
them at 0.92, which is a reflecting boundary rather than an absorbing one.

Drawing is done at grid resolution into an offscreen canvas and scaled up with drawImage,
which is both faster than shading every screen pixel and closer to what a real tank looks
like under a camera. Three views: wireframe, where only the steepest slope lights up;
shaded, where colour comes from the slope and a specular highlight is added where the
surface is flat and high; and contours, where the height is quantised into bands, which is
the pattern a real ripple tank produces through a strobe.

Sources: one sinusoidal source by default, a second one offset in position and phase on
demand, and a stone that drops a smooth cosine depression at a random point. Dragging the
pointer disturbs the surface directly.

Controls: damping (80–100 percent), force, the three draw modes, Drop a stone, Two
sources and Calm the tank. Readout: peak height, the number of cells currently above the
noise floor, mean surface displacement as an interference figure, how many sources are
live, and the current draw mode. A prefers-reduced-motion check stops the sources on
machines that ask for less motion.

Layout: nav, centred hero, the tank with its caption, a dock of three sliders and three
buttons, a readout row, then two three-cell note rows on the update, damping,
interference, edges, shading and two sources, then a footer.

Interaction: the tank is already running with one source. Visitors drag through it to draw
their own waves, drop a stone for a single ring, add a second source and watch the
interference pattern appear between them, take the damping right down to let the tank ring
for a long time, and switch to contours to see what a strobe shows.

## One-paragraph description of the intended design
A ripple tank on canvas where a 200 × 112 height field is integrated by one rule — each
cell becomes the mean of its four neighbours minus its own previous value, times a damping
factor — with reflecting edges, one or two sinusoidal sources, a stone drop, and a pointer
that disturbs the surface, drawn in three ways: a wireframe of steep slopes, a shaded
surface lit by slope with a specular highlight on flat high ground, and contour bands that
show the interference pattern the way a strobe does.

## Major visual techniques used
- The wave equation as one line: mean of four neighbours minus previous value, times
  damping, over a double-buffered Float32Array
- Reflecting edges by copying the row or column just inside the boundary at 0.92,
  which is a reflection rather than an absorbing boundary
- Drawing at grid resolution into an offscreen canvas and scaling with drawImage, which
  is faster than per-screen-pixel shading and reads as a real tank under a camera
- Colour from the slope rather than the height, so a flat grid reads as a lit surface
- A specular highlight added where the surface is flat and high, which is what makes water
  look wet
- Wireframe view as a threshold on the slope, so only the steep places light up
- Contour view as a quantised band of height, reproducing what a strobe-lit tank shows
- Two sources with a phase offset, so the interference pattern between them is a real
  consequence of the simulation
- Stone drop as a smooth cosine depression rather than a spike, so the tank does not blow
  up
- Pointer capture on the tank, so dragging continues outside it
- Mean surface displacement reported as the interference figure, computed from the same
  array the picture uses
- prefers-reduced-motion honoured by switching the sources off rather than by pausing the
  whole simulation

## Intended interaction model
Water you put your hand in. The tank runs from the moment the page opens; visitors drag
across it to make their own waves, drop a stone, add a second source and watch the
interference lines appear, lower the damping to let it ring, and switch the view between
wireframe, shaded and contours. Every number in the panel is measured off the same grid.
