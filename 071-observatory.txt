# THE DOME — an eyepiece you can aim

## Short title
Observatory

## The exact prompt used to generate this HTML file

Create a completely self-contained single-file HTML page: an observatory eyepiece you have
to aim, with a catalogue of real deep-sky objects, a field of view you choose, and altitude
and azimuth computed from latitude and hour angle. No external dependencies whatsoever —
no CDN, no web fonts, no external images, no JS libraries. All CSS and JavaScript inline,
works offline by double-clicking, responsive, valid HTML5.

Artistic direction: a dome at night. Ground #0b0a12, canvas #05050c, hairlines
rgba(242,236,245,.14), body #f2ecf5 with muted #a79caa, amber #e8c46a for the reticle and
figures and a red #e0533f for the primary action. Serif headings with italic accent words,
uppercase .2em–.4em kickers, monospace coordinates and ket-free notation.

The field is a canvas on a 900 × 520 backing store, scaled by CSS. It draws a seeded
field of nine hundred stars, each with right ascension, declination, magnitude and a tint,
projected by an orthographic rule: right ascension offset by the hour angle across, scaled
by the cosine of the declination of the field centre, and declination straight up. Stars
fainter than the field's magnitude limit are not drawn at all, and the ones that are scale
and fade by magnitude, so the field really does change character between the naked-eye and
high-power settings.

The reticle is fixed to the eyepiece, not to the sky: a crosshair, a small circle, a dashed
field stop at the radius of the field of view, and a scale bar whose label switches from
degrees to arc-minutes below one degree.

Eight catalogue objects with real coordinates — the Orion Nebula, Andromeda, Hercules
Cluster, Pleiades, Albireo, the North America Nebula, the Whirlpool and Saturn — each with
its magnitude and a one-line note. Picking one slews the mount: the field centre eases
toward the target at a fixed rate rather than jumping, and the target is drawn as a soft
glow whose size follows its brightness. Nearest object finds the closest in angular
separation.

Altitude and azimuth are computed properly from the declination, the latitude and the hour
angle, so crossing the meridian really does put a target at its highest, and the readout
reports both. Tracking advances the hour angle at the sidereal rate; turning it off blurs
the star field, which is what an untracked mount does to a photograph.

Controls: latitude (0–70), hour angle, field of view (naked eye, binoculars, wide field,
high power), Track the sky and Nearest object. Dragging the field slews the mount directly
and cancels any slew in progress. Readout: target, altitude, azimuth, field and magnitude.

Layout: nav, centred hero, the eyepiece with its coordinate overlay, a dock of three
sliders and two buttons, a four-up catalogue grid, a readout row, then two three-cell note
rows on the field, altitude, field of view, magnitude, tracking and red light, then a
footer. Catalogue collapses 4 → 2 → 1, links hidden under 780px.

Interaction: pick a target and watch the mount slew, or drag the field yourself. Change the
field of view to see the same sky as a naked eye and as a high-power view, drag the hour
angle to watch the altitude climb past the meridian, drag the latitude to see what a target
looks like from the equator or from the north, and turn tracking on for the sidereal drift.

## One-paragraph description of the intended design
An eyepiece view on canvas where a seeded field of nine hundred stars is projected by right
ascension and declination with a magnitude limit and brightness that change with the chosen
field of view, a fixed reticle and scale bar, a catalogue of eight real deep-sky objects
that slew the mount toward them at a fixed rate, altitude and azimuth properly computed
from latitude and hour angle, a track button that advances the hour angle at the sidereal
rate and blurs the field when off, and dragging to slew the mount by hand.

## Major visual techniques used
- Orthographic projection from right ascension and declination, with the across-axis
  scaled by the cosine of the field's declination, so the field has the right shape
- Magnitude limit enforced by not drawing faint stars at all, and size and alpha derived
  from magnitude, so each field of view is a different picture
- Reticle, small circle, dashed field stop and scale bar all drawn in the eyepiece's
  frame rather than the sky's, which is the whole point of an eyepiece
- Scale bar label switching between degrees and arc-minutes at one degree
- Slew as a fixed-rate ease toward the target rather than a jump or a bezier, so the
  mount behaves like a mount
- Dragging the field slews directly and cancels the slew, so two inputs cannot fight
- Target glow as a radial gradient whose radius follows the object's brightness
- Altitude and azimuth from the standard spherical relations, so meridian crossing is
  correct rather than decorative
- Tracking advancing the hour angle at 15 degrees an hour and blurring the star field
  when off, which is what an untracked mount does
- Catalogue cards carrying the real coordinates in formatted RA and Dec, so the numbers
  on the card are the numbers the sky uses
- Nearest object by true angular separation in the two axes, not by screen distance
- Coordinate overlay written every frame from the same view object the drawing uses
- A dark palette throughout, justified in the copy by dark adaptation

## Intended interaction model
An instrument you have to work. Visitors read the catalogue, pick a target and watch the
mount slew, or drag the field to aim it themselves; they choose the field of view, drag the
hour angle to see a target rise and set, drag the latitude to move the observatory, and
turn tracking on so the drift is handled for them. Nothing is aimed for you.
