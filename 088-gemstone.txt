# LAPIDARY — a stone, a cut, a refractive index

## Short title
Gemstone Cutter

## The exact prompt used to generate this HTML file

Create a completely self-contained single-file HTML page: a gemstone on a bench, cut and
lit by one moving point of light, where rays refract by Snell's law, past the critical
angle they reflect back in, and dispersion splits their colour. No external dependencies
whatsoever — no CDN, no web fonts, no external images, no JS libraries. All CSS and
JavaScript inline, works offline by double-clicking, responsive, valid HTML5.

Artistic direction: a dark room with one bulb. Ground #0c0a14, canvas #07050e, hairlines
rgba(239,234,249,.14), body #efeaf9 with muted #9d95b8, ice blue #9fd8ff as the accent and
lilac #e8b8ff for the primary action. Serif headings with italic accent words, uppercase
.2em–.4em kickers, monospace figures.

The stone is drawn as facets: sixteen crown trapezoids from the girdle to the table and
sixteen pavilion triangles down to the culet, each brightened by how much its midpoint
faces the light. The table size slider scales the crown's inner ring, and the pavilion
slider moves the culet down and the pavilion's angle with it.

The light is a single point on an orbit, and from it ten rays are walked. Each step
refracts on entering by Snell's law, and on leaving it either escapes — in which case the
exit angle determines its colour through dispersion — or reflects back in past the critical
angle, which is computed as arcsine of one over the species' index. So the rays genuinely
stay inside a well-cut diamond and leak out of a shallow one, and the colours are a
consequence of the geometry rather than a palette.

Eight species with their real refractive index, dispersion and typical yield: diamond,
ruby, sapphire, emerald, zircon, moissanite, spinel and garnet. Six cut shapes. Controls:
six cuts, eight species, pavilion angle, table, light angle, Turn the stone and Show the
fire. Readout: index, critical angle, brilliance, fire, yield and species.

Layout: nav, centred hero, the stone canvas with a live spec overlay, a dock of two chip
rows, three sliders and two buttons, a readout row, then two three-cell note rows on total
internal reflection, dispersion, yield, the pavilion, the table and fire, then a footer.

Interaction: pick a species and its index and critical angle change, so the rays behave
differently immediately. Drag the pavilion angle and watch the light stop leaking out the
back and start leaking out the side past the window; drag the light around; turn the stone
to see the facets come and go; and show the fire to see every ray leave in the colour its
own dispersion gives it.

## One-paragraph description of the intended design
A gemstone on canvas drawn as sixteen crown and sixteen pavilion facets brightened by their
facing toward a single orbiting point of light, from which ten rays are walked step by step
— refracting by Snell's law on entry, and on exit either escaping with a colour set by the
dispersion or reflecting back in past a critical angle of arcsine one over the species'
index — with eight species at their real refractive indices, six cut shapes, pavilion angle
and table sliders, and the fire mode that draws each ray in the colour its own dispersion
produces.

## Major visual techniques used
- Facets as sixteen crown trapezoids and sixteen pavilion triangles, each brightened by
  the cosine between its midpoint and the light direction
- Table size scaling the crown's inner ring, so the crown visibly re-forms
- Pavilion angle moving the culet down the axis by (48 − pavilion), so the cut is a
  parameter and not a picture
- Rays traced stepwise, refracting by Snell's law at each interface
- Critical angle computed as arcsine of one over the index, and past it the ray reflects
  instead of escaping, which is why a well-cut stone keeps its light
- Dispersion colour from the exit angle's deviation, with red bent least and violet most
- Brilliance as a window around the ideal pavilion angle for the species, so the readout
  is a real measurement of the cut
- Critical angle and index printed as a live spec overlay from the same numbers the rays
  use
- Girdle outline as a closed path of the cut's own proportions, so each cut shape is a
  different outline
- Light as a radial gradient glow at the end of an orbit set by the angle slider
- Stroked stone body as a gradient through the species' own hue

## Intended interaction model
A stone to cut and to light. Visitors pick a species and the physics changes, drag the
pavilion angle and watch the light stop leaking out the back and start leaking out the side,
widen or narrow the table, move the light around the stone, turn the stone to see the facets
come and go, and show the fire to see each ray leave in the colour its own dispersion gives
it. Nothing on screen is a preset: the rays are traced and the colours are computed.
